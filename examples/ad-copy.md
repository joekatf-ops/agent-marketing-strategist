## 2. The job

Move the belief that more pockets is the same as being able to see the cable.

## 3. Proof, claims and production needs

- `EVD-PROD-001`, verified product fact: transparent mesh divider and six elastic cable loops
- `EVD-MKT-022`, market evidence: 21 reviewed comments mention digging, tangles or forgotten cables
- `EVD-CLAIM-006`, approved wording: "See your cables at a glance."
- Prohibited: waterproof, indestructible, fits every charger, prevents forgetting, customer results,
  any time-saving claim beyond what the video frame shows
- People, assets and location: none additional. Copy runs against the existing comparison video

## 7. Primary text A, Long version

Same six cables. Two very different ways to find one.

If you carry charging gear between home and a shared workspace, you know the specific moment. The
laptop is nearly flat, the meeting starts in three minutes, and you are holding a pouch that
absolutely contains the right cable. You just cannot see it. So you start pulling things out onto the
desk, which is how a thirty second job becomes a two minute one in front of other people.

The instinct is to buy something with more compartments. It rarely helps, because a compartment is
an opaque place a cable can be. Adding compartments adds places to check.

SnapGrid takes the opposite approach. A transparent mesh divider runs the length of the pouch and six
elastic loops hold each cable in a fixed position. Unzip it once and every cable is visible at the
same time. Nothing is buried under anything else, and nothing has to be removed to find out what is
underneath.

We shot it as a straight comparison because it is the kind of claim that should not need arguing.
Same six cables, same starting position, same locked camera. One side gets searched. One side gets
looked at.

That is the whole product. Not more space, not more pockets. You can see what you packed.

See your cables at a glance.

*225 words.*

## 11. Primary text B, Long version

Pockets are not visibility.

Almost every cable organiser competes on compartments. More pockets, more elastic, more zips. It is a
reasonable-sounding answer to the wrong question, because a compartment is an opaque place a cable
can be. Adding compartments adds places you have to check before you find the one you want.

The actual problem is that you cannot see what you packed. You know the cable is in there. That is
never the issue. The issue is that finding it means taking other things out first, usually at the
exact moment you have no time to do it.

SnapGrid is built around that instead. A transparent mesh divider runs the length of the pouch and
six elastic loops hold each cable in a fixed position. One unzip and every cable is visible
simultaneously. Nothing sits underneath anything else, so nothing has to be moved to reveal what is
below it.

We shot it as a direct comparison against an ordinary pouch. Same six cables, same starting position,
same locked camera, no cuts inside the take. One side gets searched and one side gets read at a
glance. It is a claim that should be settled in front of you rather than argued.

Not more space. Not more pockets. You can see what you packed.

See your cables at a glance.

*221 words.*

## 12. Headlines

1. Same six cables. One you can see
2. Pockets are not visibility
3. Stop emptying the pouch to find the cable
4. Every cable in a fixed position

Four rather than five. A fifth was drafted, "The cable organiser you can read at a glance," and cut
because it selects the same prospect as headline 4 through the same mechanism, so it added length
without adding reach. Each of the four selects differently: 1 through the comparison, 2 through the
category reframe, 3 through the felt moment, 4 through the mechanism.

## 15. Rationale

Two routes because a reader who ignores the situational opening may still respond to a category
claim, and vice versa. Route A enters through the felt moment at SLA, which is where the awareness
model puts a reader who has a pouch and is dissatisfied with it. Route B enters through the reframe,
which is the same argument stated as a proposition.

Neither route makes a time claim. The video shows a four second retrieval in frame, and the copy
deliberately does not generalise that into a speed benefit, because one recorded take is not a
measured average.

Both first lines survive truncation. Route A's first line is the hook verbatim, which keeps the video
and the copy expressing one idea. Route B's is four words.

The proof sits before the ask in all six versions. The long versions name the comparison method,
matched cables and locked camera, because a comparison that does not state its conditions is not
proof.

Structure per `references/05-copy-craft.md`: PAS for route A with a single agitation line, since the
video already carried the cost and repeating it in copy would be padding. 4P for route B, where the
picture is the visible layout and the proof is the filmed comparison.
